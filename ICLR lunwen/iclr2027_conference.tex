\begin{abstract}
Hypergraph-based Retrieval-Augmented Generation(RAG) retrieves $n$-ary relational facts, but generally ignores the hierarchical organization of documents, through which information is conveyed across levels. This omission hinders the assembly of coherent evidence when answers require both global context and local facts. To address this limitation, we introduce a new framework called Asymmetric Indexing-based Hierarchical Hyper-RAG(AI-H\textsuperscript{2}RAG). It consists of a comprehensive pipeline, including: (i) hierarchical hypergraph construction, (ii) asymmetric indexing, and (iii) structure-aware retrieval and generation. It constructs a hierarchical hypergraph to connect global context with local facts, propagates information across its levels through asymmetric indexing, and aligns each query with the indexed hierarchical hypergraph to retrieve evidence in a coarse-to-fine manner for answer generation. Experiments across eight document question-answering benchmarks demonstrate that AI-H\textsuperscript{2}RAG outperforms standard, graph-based, and hypergraph-based RAG baselines in answer quality. Notably, our method achieves the strongest performance across questions requiring different evidence depths in multi-stage Mechanical question answering, while maintaining retrieval latency comparable to the strongest baselines.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) augments generation with external evidence retrieved at inference time \citep{Lewis2020RAG,Guu2020REALM}. By decoupling knowledge from model parameters, it supports source-grounded answers and updates to evolving corpora without retraining. These properties are particularly valuable in specialized domains, where answers depend on document-specific terminology, constraints, and time-varying evidence \citep{Yang2025KBSLLMKB}. Accordingly, RAG has emerged as a widely adopted paradigm in both academic research and industrial applications.

Current RAG methodologies broadly retrieve evidence from documents, graphs, or hypergraphs. Document-based RAG independently indexes text chunks and retrieves them through learned query--passage similarity \citep{Izacard2023Atlas}.Graph-based RAG organizes entities and relations into structured retrievers, connecting distributed evidence through entity paths or aggregating support within communities \citep{Jin2024LLMGraphs,Zhong2023KGConstruction}. Hypergraph-based RAG further represents high-order relations among multiple entities or facts \citep{Feng2019HGNN,Feng2026HyperRAG}. However, none of these paradigms explicitly captures the hierarchical document structure.

Documents naturally organize evidence across several levels, including topics, chunks, sentences, and entities. Information is transmitted across these levels: topics define the scope of related chunks, chunks provide context for sentences, and sentences ground entities and their relations. Answering complex questions therefore requires jointly integrating evidence from the document hierarchy, rather than relying on isolated evidence at a single granularity. Hypergraphs are well suited to this setting because a hyperedge can directly connect multiple heterogeneous elements across levels, capturing the many-to-many dependencies among topics, chunks, sentences, and entities \citep{Chien2022AllSet}. Such a unified representation preserves both broad contextual scope and fine-grained factual specificity throughout the retrieval process.

Motivated by these insights, we design AI-H\textsuperscript{2}RAG, a hierarchical hypergraph RAG framework with asymmetric indexing. It is built upon three key steps. First, we propose a hierarchical hypergraph construction method, which builds three-layer nested hypergraphs to hierarchically represent the document structure. According to the document hierarchy, we represent the document as topic--chunk, chunk--sentence, and sentence--entity hypergraphs. This construction clearly supports information propagation across different levels of the document. Second, we introduce an asymmetric indexing mechanism. Within the constructed hierarchical hypergraph structure, it propagates final parent representations downward as contextual guidance, while aggregating raw fine-grained child representations upward as factual support for higher-level semantics. Lastly, we develop a structure-aware retrieval and generation strategy that aligns each query with the indexed hierarchical hypergraph to retrieve evidence from coarse to fine granularity for answer generation.

To validate the effectiveness, We conduct experiments in multiple knowledge-intensive document QA benchmarks \citep{Guo2025LightRAG}, spanning physics, pathology, mix and five machine-tool domains, including Mechanical. Results demonstrate that AI-H\textsuperscript{2}RAG outperforms a range of previous RAG methods, and achieves the strongest performance across questions requiring different evidence depths in multi-stage Mechanical question answering, while maintaining retrieval latency comparable to the strongest baselines, demonstrating its great potential for practical, real-world applications.

Our contributions are the following:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We propose AI-H²RAG, a hierarchical hypergraph RAG framework that models documents as three nested hypergraph layers, bridging global context and local facts to answer multi-granularity evidence-based questions.
    \item We introduce an asymmetric indexing mechanism that propagates final parent representations downward as contextual guidance to guide fine-grained representations, while aggregating raw fine-grained child representations upward as factual support for coarse-level semantics.
    \item We develop a structure-aware retrieval and generation strategy that retrieves coarse-to-fine evidence by aligning queries with the hierarchical hypergraph for answer generation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related-work}

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{RAG}
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) couples a retriever with a language model to provide external evidence for knowledge-intensive tasks \citep{Asai2024SelfRAG}. Scaled retrieval and self-reflective variants extend this paradigm \citep{Borgeaud2022RETRO}. Recent surveys summarize the rapidly expanding RAG literature \citep{Gao2024RAGSurvey,Huang2026RAGSurvey}. Dense retrieval improves evidence access through learned query and passage representations. Query rewriting and hypothetical-document expansion improve underspecified retrieval inputs \citep{Ma2023QueryRewriting}. Question-answering benchmarks such as SQuAD and Natural Questions evaluate evidence-grounded answering \citep{Rajpurkar2016SQuAD,Kwiatkowski2019NaturalQuestions}. RAG evaluation also assesses retrieval grounding and generated responses \citep{Es2024RAGAS}. However, conventional RAG usually indexes passages independently, limiting its ability to coordinate document scope with local factual evidence \citep{Adjali2024MultiLevelRAG,Liu2024LostMiddle}.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Graph-based RAG}
Graph-based RAG connects related evidence through entities, triples, and communities \citep{Pan2024LLMKG,Peng2025GraphRAGSurvey}. GraphRAG supports local and global query-focused summarization through entity graphs and community reports \citep{Edge2025GraphRAG}. LightRAG improves retrieval efficiency with dual-level graph retrieval \citep{Guo2025LightRAG}. Knowledge-graph methods also support relation-path reasoning for complex question answering \citep{Lan2023ComplexKBQA,Xu2024KGRAGCSQA}. Graph neural encoders aggregate neighborhood information through convolution or sampled message passing \citep{Kipf2017GCN,Hamilton2017GraphSAGE}. Attention-based graph encoders provide an alternative mechanism for weighting neighborhood information \citep{Velickovic2018GAT,Wu2021GNNSurvey}. Entity linking resolves surface mentions to canonical graph nodes \citep{Hoffart2011AIDA,Sevgili2022EntityLinkingSurvey}, while entity-aware contextual encoders provide learned entity representations \citep{Yamada2020LUKE}. Nevertheless, these methods primarily organize pairwise relations, entity neighborhoods, or coarse summaries, without explicitly constructing the document's hierarchical structure for multi-granularity retrieval needed for QA.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Hypergraph-based RAG}
Hypergraphs generalize ordinary graphs by allowing each hyperedge to connect multiple vertices, thereby representing high-order dependencies beyond pairwise links \citep{Feng2019HGNN,Gao2023HGNNPlus}. Set-based hypergraph architectures provide a complementary formulation for aggregating variable-sized neighborhoods \citep{Chien2022AllSet}. Hyper-RAG extends this principle to retrieval-augmented generation through multi-entity hypergraph organization \citep{Feng2026HyperRAG}. Existing hypergraph RAG methods, however, typically construct hyperedges over entities or other single-level units. They do not explore hypergraphs as a means of explicitly representing the hierarchical structure of documents. Therefore, we propose AI-H\textsuperscript{2}RAG, which hierarchically represents document structure with hypergraphs to support multi-granularity evidence retrieval.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

In this section, we introduce the proposed method AI-H\textsuperscript{2}RAG, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:Caschyper_overall_framework}, including hierarchical hypergraph construction, asymmetric indexing, and structure-aware retrieval and generation. 

\subsection{Hierarchical Hypergraph Construction}
\label{sec:hierarchical_hypergraph}

Documents organize evidence across topics, chunks, sentences, and entities. To represent document levels, we propose a hierarchical hypergraph construction method that includes multi-level hypergraph extraction, cross-level membership mapping, and raw representation initialization. The overall architecture is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:hypergraph_construction}.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Multi-level Hypergraph Extraction.}
Given a document corpus $\mathcal{D}=\{d_n\}_{n=1}^{N_{\mathcal{D}}}$, we decompose each document into chunks, sentences, and entities. Across the corpus, these units form the collections $\mathcal{C}=\{c_i\}_{i=1}^{N}$, $\mathcal{S}=\{s_j\}_{j=1}^{J}$, and $\mathcal{U}=\{u_m\}_{m=1}^{M}$, respectively. Here, we use sentences to refer to sentence-level textual units, and employ the term entities broadly to encompass both conventional entities and salient semantic elements, including actions, conditions, events, and relations. During entity extraction, we additionally assign each extracted entity an importance score.  The prompts for extracting sentences and entities are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:sentence_extraction_prompt} and Appendix~\ref{app:entity_extraction_prompt}. To represent these textual units and their relationships, a hypergraph is defined as $\mathcal{H}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})$, where $\mathcal{V}$ is the vertex set and $\mathcal{E}$ is the hyperedge set.

We first construct a fuzzy topic--chunk hypergraph. To obtain topic representations, we induce a set of latent topic anchors $\mathcal{T}=\{t_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$ and a fuzzy topic--chunk membership matrix $\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{T}\to\mathrm{C}}=[m_{ki}^{\mathrm{T}\to\mathrm{C}}]\in[0,1]^{K\times N}$ from the chunk set $\mathcal{C}$, where $m_{ki}^{\mathrm{T}\to\mathrm{C}}\in[0,1]$ denotes the membership strength of chunk $c_i$ with respect to topic $t_k$. Unlike hard topic partitioning, this fuzzy assignment preserves cross-topic semantic associations by allowing a chunk to participate in multiple topic hyperedges. The detailed fuzzy-topic induction procedure is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:latent_topic_construction}.

Each topic $t_k$ serves as the semantic anchor of a topic-induced hyperedge
$e_k^{\mathrm{T}\to\mathrm{C}}=\{c_i\in\mathcal{C}\mid m_{ki}^{\mathrm{T}\to\mathrm{C}}>0\}$.
Accordingly, with
$\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{T}\to\mathrm{C}}
=
\{e_k^{\mathrm{T}\to\mathrm{C}}\}_{k=1}^{K}$,
we define the topic--chunk hypergraph as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{T}\to\mathrm{C}}
=
\left(
\mathcal{C},
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{T}\to\mathrm{C}}
\right).
\end{equation}

We further construct two containment-based hypergraph layers:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{C}\to\mathrm{S}}
=
\left(
\mathcal{S},
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{C}\to\mathrm{S}}
\right),
\qquad
\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{S}\to\mathrm{U}}
=
\left(
\mathcal{U},
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{S}\to\mathrm{U}}
\right).
\label{eq:containment_hypergraphs}
\end{equation}
where each chunk $c_i$ is associated with a hyperedge $e_i^{\mathrm{C}\to\mathrm{S}}$ that connects its contained sentences, and each sentence $s_j$ is associated with a hyperedge $e_j^{\mathrm{S}\to\mathrm{U}}$ that connects its extracted entities. Consequently, the three nested hypergraph layers represent four document levels: topics, chunks, sentences, and entities.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Cross-level Membership Mapping.}
We preserve membership relations between adjacent document levels through
three incidence mappings:
$\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{T}\to\mathrm{C}}=[m_{ki}^{\mathrm{T}\to\mathrm{C}}]
\in[0,1]^{K\times N}$,
$\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{C}\to\mathrm{S}}
=[m_{ij}^{\mathrm{C}\to\mathrm{S}}]\in\{0,1\}^{N\times J}$, and
$\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{S}\to\mathrm{U}}
=[m_{jm}^{\mathrm{S}\to\mathrm{U}}]\in[0,1]^{J\times M}$,
where $N$, $K$, $J$, and $M$ denote the numbers of chunks, topics, sentences, and entities, respectively. The topic--chunk mapping retains fuzzy membership weights, whereas the remaining mappings encode document containment relations:
\begin{equation}
m_{ij}^{\mathrm{C}\to\mathrm{S}}
=
\mathbb{I}[s_j \subset c_i],
\qquad
m_{jm}^{\mathrm{S}\to\mathrm{U}}
=
\rho_m\,\mathbb{I}[u_m \subset s_j],
\label{eq:containment_membership_mapping}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_m\in[0,1]$ is the importance score assigned to entity $u_m$ during entity extraction. These mappings allow retrieval to move structurally in both directions. A fine-grained entity can be traced back to its enclosing sentence, chunk, and topic, while a coarse topic can look for local facts that support it.


\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Raw Representation Initialization.}
Before cross-level information propagation, we initialize an $\ell_2$-normalized raw representation for every document unit. Let $\operatorname{Enc}_{\mathrm{bge}}(\cdot)$ denote the BGE-M3 text encoder and $\operatorname{norm}(\cdot)$ denote $\ell_2$ normalization. For chunks, sentences, and entities, we obtain
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_{c_i}^{(\mathrm{raw})}
=
\operatorname{norm}\!\left(
\operatorname{Enc}_{\mathrm{bge}}(c_i)
\right),
\hspace{1.2em}
\mathbf{h}_{s_j}^{(\mathrm{raw})}
=
\operatorname{norm}\!\left(
\operatorname{Enc}_{\mathrm{bge}}(s_j)
\right),
\hspace{1.2em}
\mathbf{h}_{u_m}^{(\mathrm{raw})}
=
\operatorname{norm}\!\left(
\operatorname{Enc}_{\mathrm{bge}}(u_m)
\right).
\label{eq:raw_representations}
\end{equation}

The initial representation of topic $t_k$ is obtained by aggregating its member chunks according to their fuzzy memberships:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_{t_k}^{(\mathrm{raw})}
=
\operatorname{norm}\!\left(
\frac{
\sum_{c_i\in e_k^{\mathrm{T}\to\mathrm{C}}}
m_{ki}^{\mathrm{T}\to\mathrm{C}}\mathbf{h}_{c_i}^{(\mathrm{raw})}
}{
\sum_{c_i\in e_k^{\mathrm{T}\to\mathrm{C}}}m_{ki}^{\mathrm{T}\to\mathrm{C}}
}
\right).
\label{eq:topic_raw_representation}
\end{equation}

These representations preserve the original information available at each level.

\subsection{Asymmetric Indexing}

After constructing the hierarchical hypergraph, we introduce an asymmetric indexing mechanism that constructs final index representations across document levels through cross-level information propagation. The mechanism comprises asymmetric propagation and layer-wise
index updates.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Asymmetric Propagation.}
For notational convenience, we define similarity-weighted fusion between a reference representation $\mathbf{r}$ and an input set $\mathcal{Z}=\{\mathbf{z}_i\}_{i=1}^{L}$ as
\begin{equation}
\alpha_i =
\frac{\exp(\mathbf{r}^{\top}\mathbf{z}_i/\kappa)}
{\sum_{j=1}^{L}\exp(\mathbf{r}^{\top}\mathbf{z}_j/\kappa)},
\qquad
\operatorname{Fuse}_{\mathrm{sim}}(\mathbf{r},\mathcal{Z})
=
\operatorname{norm}\!\left(
\sum_{i=1}^{L}\alpha_i\mathbf{z}_i
\right),
\label{eq:similarity_weighted_fusion}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa$ is a temperature parameter.

For an element $x$, let $\operatorname{Par}(x)$ and $\operatorname{Child}(x)$ denote its associated elements at the immediately coarser and finer levels, respectively. Each element $x\in\mathcal{C}\cup\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{U}$ is then updated by
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_{x}^{(\mathrm{final})}
=
\operatorname{Fuse}_{\mathrm{sim}}\!\left(
\mathbf{h}_{x}^{(\mathrm{raw})},
\left\{
\mathbf{h}_{x}^{(\mathrm{raw})},
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{par}}^{(\mathrm{final})}(x),
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{child}}^{(\mathrm{raw})}(x)
\right\}
\right).
\label{eq:asymmetric_propagation}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{par}}^{(\mathrm{final})}(x)$ and $\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{child}}^{(\mathrm{raw})}(x)$ denote the aggregated final parent context and raw child evidence of $x$, respectively. When the corresponding associated set is non-empty, they are computed as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{par}}^{(\mathrm{final})}(x)
=
\operatorname{norm}\!\left(
\frac{
\sum_{p\in\operatorname{Par}(x)}
w_{p,x}\mathbf{h}_{p}^{(\mathrm{final})}
}{
\sum_{p\in\operatorname{Par}(x)} w_{p,x}
}
\right),
\quad
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{child}}^{(\mathrm{raw})}(x)
=
\operatorname{norm}\!\left(
\frac{
\sum_{y\in\operatorname{Child}(x)}
w_{x,y}\mathbf{h}_{y}^{(\mathrm{raw})}
}{
\sum_{y\in\operatorname{Child}(x)} w_{x,y}
}
\right).
\label{eq:parent_child_aggregation}
\end{equation}
The weights are inherited from the hierarchical membership mappings: $w_{t_k,c_i}=m_{ki}^{\mathrm{T}\to\mathrm{C}}$, $w_{c_i,s_j}=m_{ij}^{\mathrm{C}\to\mathrm{S}}$, and $w_{s_j,u_m}=m_{jm}^{\mathrm{S}\to\mathrm{U}}$. Only elements with nonzero membership or containment weights participate in the corresponding aggregation.

The raw representation $\mathbf{h}_{x}^{(\mathrm{raw})}$ serves as the fusion reference and is also included explicitly in the fusion inputs, thereby anchoring the resulting representation to the original semantics of $x$. The propagation is asymmetric: final representations from the coarser level provide downward contextual guidance, whereas only raw representations from the finer level provide upward factual support. Thus, contextual information received by a finer-level element is never reused as factual evidence at a coarser level. For entity elements, which have no finer-level children, $\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{child}}^{(\mathrm{raw})}(x)$ is omitted from the fusion inputs.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Layer-wise Index Update.}
Starting from the raw representations initialized in \textbf{Raw Representation Initialization}, we use the cross-level incidence mappings $\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{T}\to\mathrm{C}}$, $\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{C}\to\mathrm{S}}$, and $\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{S}\to\mathrm{U}}$ to construct the final hierarchical index representations. No additional propagation is performed at the topic level; the initialized topic representations are directly retained as the final representations: $\mathbf{h}_{t_k}^{(\mathrm{final})}=\mathbf{h}_{t_k}^{(\mathrm{raw})}$.

The chunk, sentence, and entity representations are updated sequentially in a coarse-to-fine order according to \Eqref{eq:asymmetric_propagation}. Each chunk integrates final topic context with raw sentence evidence, and each sentence integrates final chunk context with importance-weighted raw entity evidence. Each entity integrates its raw representation with the final context of its parent sentence, without a child-evidence term. This procedure yields the final topic, chunk, sentence, and entity representations $\{\mathbf{h}_{t_k}^{(\mathrm{final})}\}_{k=1}^{K}$, $\{\mathbf{h}_{c_i}^{(\mathrm{final})}\}_{i=1}^{N}$, $\{\mathbf{h}_{s_j}^{(\mathrm{final})}\}_{j=1}^{J}$, and $\{\mathbf{h}_{u_m}^{(\mathrm{final})}\}_{m=1}^{M}$, respectively. Appendix~\ref{app:asymmetric_indexing_details} provides the corresponding layer-specific aggregation and update formulas. We further find that
incorporating hierarchical context during propagation helps the resulting entity representations distinguish the same entity when it carries different meanings across document contexts; a diagnostic analysis is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:same_entity_different_sense_disambiguation}.


\subsection{Structure-aware Retrieval and Generation}

To retrieve evidence at the granularity required by a query, we develop a structure-aware retrieval and generation strategy comprising query alignment, hierarchical evidence retrieval, and evidence-grounded generation.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Query Alignment.}
Given the hierarchically indexed document structure, we align each query with the corresponding document levels. For a query $q$, we first use an LLM to construct an expanded textual query $\tilde{q}$ and extract its entities $\mathcal{U}_{q}=\{u_{\ell}^{q}\}_{\ell=1}^{L_q}$. Each extracted entity $u_{\ell}^{q}$ is assigned an importance score $\rho_{\ell}^{q}\in[0,1]$. Following the raw-representation initialization in \Eqref{eq:raw_representations}, we encode $\tilde{q}$ and its entities as $\mathbf{h}_{q}^{(\mathrm{raw})}$ and $\{\mathbf{h}_{u_{\ell}^{q}}^{(\mathrm{raw})}\}_{\ell=1}^{L_q}$, respectively.

We use the raw query representation to activate relevant topic index representations:
\begin{equation}
a_{qk}
=
{\mathbf{h}_{q}^{(\mathrm{raw})}}^{\top}
\mathbf{h}_{t_k}^{(\mathrm{final})},
\qquad
\mathcal{A}(q)
=
\operatorname{TopP}_{t_k\in\mathcal{T}} a_{qk},
\label{eq:query_topic_activation}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{A}(q)$ is the set of activated topics. Then, we construct query-side representations that correspond to the chunk, sentence, and entity levels of the hierarchical index: $\mathbf{h}_{q,\mathrm{C}}^{(\mathrm{final})}$, $\mathbf{h}_{q,\mathrm{S}}^{(\mathrm{final})}$, and $\{\mathbf{h}_{u_{\ell}^{q}}^{(\mathrm{final})}\}_{\ell=1}^{L_q}$. To this end, we construct two query signals, including a topic-level query signal and an entity-level query signal:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{topic}}^{(\mathrm{final})}(q)
=
\operatorname{norm}\!\left(
\frac{
\sum_{t_k\in\mathcal{A}(q)}
\beta_{qk}\mathbf{h}_{t_k}^{(\mathrm{final})}
}{
\sum_{t_k\in\mathcal{A}(q)}
\beta_{qk}
}
\right),
\quad
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{unit}}^{(\mathrm{raw})}(q)
=
\operatorname{norm}\!\left(
\frac{
\sum_{\ell=1}^{L_q}
\rho_{\ell}^{q}\mathbf{h}_{u_{\ell}^{q}}^{(\mathrm{raw})}
}{
\sum_{\ell=1}^{L_q}\rho_{\ell}^{q}
}
\right),
\label{eq:query_context_evidence_aggregation}
\end{equation}
where $\beta_{qk}=\exp(a_{qk}/\tau_q)$ is the temperature-scaled weight of the activation score $a_{qk}$, $\tau_q$ is a temperature parameter, and $\rho_{\ell}^{q}$ denotes the importance score of query entity $u_{\ell}^{q}$. Following the asymmetric fusion principle used for index construction, we construct query-side representations aligned with the chunk, sentence, and entity levels of the document index. Let $q_{\mathrm{C}}$ and $q_{\mathrm{S}}$ denote the query states whose final representations are $\mathbf{h}_{q,\mathrm{C}}^{(\mathrm{final})}$ and $\mathbf{h}_{q,\mathrm{S}}^{(\mathrm{final})}$, respectively. For each target $x\in\{q_{\mathrm{C}},q_{\mathrm{S}},u_{\ell}^{q}\}$, we write the update as:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_{x}^{(\mathrm{final})}
=
\operatorname{Fuse}_{\mathrm{sim}}\!\left(
\mathbf{r}_{x},
\mathcal{Z}_{x}
\right),
\label{eq:query_hierarchical_alignment}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{r}_{x}$ is the raw reference representation and
$\mathcal{Z}_{x}$ is the corresponding fusion input set:
\begin{equation}
\left(
\mathbf{r}_{x},
\mathcal{Z}_{x}
\right)
=
\begin{cases}
\left(
\mathbf{h}_{q}^{(\mathrm{raw})},
\left\{
\mathbf{h}_{q}^{(\mathrm{raw})},
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{topic}}^{(\mathrm{final})}(q)
\right\}
\right),
& x=q_{\mathrm{C}}, \\[0.5em]
\left(
\mathbf{h}_{q}^{(\mathrm{raw})},
\left\{
\mathbf{h}_{q}^{(\mathrm{raw})},
\mathbf{h}_{q,\mathrm{C}}^{(\mathrm{final})},
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{unit}}^{(\mathrm{raw})}(q)
\right\}
\right),
& x=q_{\mathrm{S}}, \\[0.5em]
\left(
\mathbf{h}_{u_{\ell}^{q}}^{(\mathrm{raw})},
\left\{
\mathbf{h}_{u_{\ell}^{q}}^{(\mathrm{raw})},
\mathbf{h}_{q,\mathrm{S}}^{(\mathrm{final})}
\right\}
\right),
& x=u_{\ell}^{q},\quad \ell=1,\ldots,L_q .
\end{cases}
\label{eq:query_alignment_inputs}
\end{equation}

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Hierarchical Evidence Retrieval.}
Retrieval follows the same document hierarchy from coarse to fine. The activated topics first restrict the candidate chunks through the topic--chunk mapping:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C}(q)
=
\left\{
c_i \in \mathcal{C}
\;\middle|\;
\exists\, t_k \in \mathcal{A}(q),
\; m_{ki}^{\mathrm{T}\to\mathrm{C}} > 0
\right\}.
\end{equation}
We perform coarse chunk recall by matching each candidate chunk with the chunk-aligned query representation:
\begin{equation}
r_{c_i}
=
{\mathbf{h}_{q,\mathrm{C}}^{(\mathrm{final})}}^{\top}
\mathbf{h}_{c_i}^{(\mathrm{final})}.
\label{eq:chunk_retrieval_score}
\end{equation}
The top-ranked chunks define a sentence candidate set through $\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{C}\to\mathrm{S}}$. We subsequently rerank candidate sentences using three complementary relevance signals:
\begin{equation}
r_{j,\mathrm{C}}
=
{\mathbf{h}_{q}^{(\mathrm{raw})}}^{\top}
\mathbf{h}_{c_i}^{(\mathrm{final})},
\qquad
r_{j,\mathrm{S}}
=
{\mathbf{h}_{q}^{(\mathrm{raw})}}^{\top}
\mathbf{h}_{s_j}^{(\mathrm{final})},
\qquad
r_{j,\mathrm{U}}
=
\frac{1}{L_q}
\sum_{\ell=1}^{L_q}
\max_{u_m\in\mathcal{U}(s_j)}
{\mathbf{h}_{u_{\ell}^{q}}^{(\mathrm{final})}}^{\top}
\mathbf{h}_{u_m}^{(\mathrm{final})},
\label{eq:sentence_relevance_signals}
\end{equation}
where $c_i$ is the retrieved chunk associated with candidate sentence $s_j$, and $\mathcal{U}(s_j)=\{u_m\in\mathcal{U}\mid m_{jm}^{\mathrm{S}\to\mathrm{U}}>0\}$ denotes the set of entities extracted from $s_j$. Appendix~\ref{app:query_scoring_comparison} justifies our choice of query
representations for the chunk-level and sentence-level signals in sentence reranking. The three signals measure chunk-level context relevance, direct sentence relevance, and entity-level relevance, respectively. They are adaptively combined for fine-grained evidence selection:
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}
=
\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(
\frac{
[r_{j,\mathrm{C}},r_{j,\mathrm{S}},r_{j,\mathrm{U}}]
}{
\tau_{\mathrm r}
}
\right),
\qquad
R_j
=
\sum_{g\in\{\mathrm{C},\mathrm{S},\mathrm{U}\}}
\omega_{j,g}r_{j,g},
\label{eq:sentence_reranking_score}
\end{equation}
where $\tau_{\mathrm r}$ is a temperature parameter. The highest-ranked sentences provide precise local evidence. Starting from their extracted entities, we further expand to linked sentences that share relevant entities, thereby recovering multi-hop evidence beyond the initially retrieved chunks. Details of multi-hop evidence expansion procedure are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:entity_mediated_expansion}.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Evidence-Grounded Generation.}
Let $\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{C}}(q)$ denote the top-ranked chunk evidence and $\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{S}}(q)$ denote the reranked and expanded sentence evidence. We generate the answer as
\begin{equation}
\hat{y}
=
\operatorname{LLM}\!\left(
q,\,
\operatorname{Text}\!\left(\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{C}}(q)\right),\,
\operatorname{Text}\!\left(\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{S}}(q)\right)
\right),
\label{eq:evidence_grounded_generation}
\end{equation}
where chunk evidence provides document-level context and sentence evidence provides fine-grained factual support.

\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}
\subsection{Experimental Setup}

We evaluate eight document QA datasets spanning five machine-tool and three general domains. The Mechanical evaluation uses three evidence-depth stages: Stage~1 requires direct evidence, Stage~2 requires one intermediate connection, and Stage~3 requires two connected evidence links. Baselines include an LLM without retrieval, dense RAG \citep{Karpukhin2020DPR}, GraphRAG \citep{Edge2025GraphRAG}, LightRAG \citep{Guo2025LightRAG}, HippoRAG~2 \citep{Gutierrez2025HippoRAG2}, and Hyper-RAG \citep{Feng2026HyperRAG}. Within each comparison and evaluation run, all methods share the documents, questions, reference evidence, generator, and evaluation protocol. GPT-4o-mini generates questions and judges answers against reference evidence on six criteria scored from 0 to 100. We conduct five independent evaluation runs. In each run, we randomly generate a distinct 50-question evaluation set for each dataset. We report the mean $\pm$ standard deviation across the five run-level mean scores; the average score is the mean across the six criteria. Appendix~\ref{app:experimental_setup} provides dataset statistics, preprocessing, and the detailed evaluation protocol; Appendix~\ref{app:llm_judge_scoring_prompt} reports the judging prompt. Appendices~\ref{app:expert_blind_evaluation} and~\ref{app:key_evidence_chain_retrieval} report the expert blind evaluation and evidence-chain retrieval analysis, respectively.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

AI-H\textsuperscript{2}RAG organizes document evidence as nested topic--chunk, chunk--sentence, and sentence--entity hypergraphs, constructs an asymmetric bidirectional index, and applies cascaded multi-granularity enhancement to queries. Across eight document QA datasets, five LLM backbones, and three Mechanical question stages, it achieves the highest average performance. AI-H\textsuperscript{2}RAG achieves the highest answer-quality score while retaining retrieval latency comparable to Hyper-RAG. These results support the effectiveness of cascaded multi-granularity retrieval for answer quality and evidence coverage.

\section{Latent Topic Construction from Chunk Similarity}
\label{app:latent_topic_construction}

This appendix details how the latent topic anchors
$\mathcal{T}=\{t_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$ and the fuzzy topic--chunk membership
matrix $\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{T}\to\mathrm{C}}
=[m_{ki}^{\mathrm{T}\to\mathrm{C}}]\in[0,1]^{K\times N}$ are induced from the chunk set
$\mathcal{C}=\{c_i\}_{i=1}^{N}$. As defined in
\Eqref{eq:raw_representations}, $\mathbf{h}_{c_i}^{(\mathrm{raw})}
\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ denotes the $\ell_2$-normalized raw representation of
chunk $c_i$. The pairwise semantic similarity between chunks is therefore
computed by
\begin{equation}
s_{ij}={\mathbf{h}_{c_i}^{(\mathrm{raw})}}^{\top}\mathbf{h}_{c_j}^{(\mathrm{raw})},
\quad i\neq j.
\end{equation}

To retain only informative semantic connections, we compute a dynamic
threshold from the upper-triangular similarity values:
\begin{equation}
\tau=\operatorname{mean}\left(\{s_{ij}\mid i<j\}\right)
+0.5\operatorname{std}\left(\{s_{ij}\mid i<j\}\right).
\end{equation}

We then construct a weighted undirected chunk-similarity graph
$\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R})$, where
\begin{equation}
(c_i,c_j)\in\mathcal{R}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ s_{ij}>\tau,
\qquad w_{ij}=s_{ij}.
\end{equation}

Leiden community detection is applied to $\mathcal{G}$ with edge weights
$w_{ij}$ and resolution parameter $1.0$, producing a partition
$\{\mathcal{C}_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$ of the chunks \citep{Traag2019Leiden}.
Each community $\mathcal{C}_k$ induces one latent topic $t_k$; together,
these topics form $\mathcal{T}$. The representation of topic anchor $t_k$
is the normalized centroid of its member chunk representations:
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\mu}_k=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}_k|}
\sum_{c_i\in\mathcal{C}_k}\mathbf{h}_{c_i}^{(\mathrm{raw})},
\qquad
\mathbf{t}_k=\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}_k}{|\boldsymbol{\mu}_k|_2}.
\end{equation}

Although Leiden provides a primary topic assignment, we make the
topic--chunk memberships fuzzy to preserve cross-topic associations. Given
a fuzzy-membership threshold $\eta$, we define
\begin{equation}
m_{ki}^{\mathrm{T}\to\mathrm{C}}=
\begin{cases}
1, & c_i\in\mathcal{C}_k,\\
{\mathbf{h}_{c_i}^{(\mathrm{raw})}}^{\top}\mathbf{t}_k,
& c_i\notin\mathcal{C}_k\ \text{and}\
{\mathbf{h}_{c_i}^{(\mathrm{raw})}}^{\top}\mathbf{t}_k>\eta,\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

Collecting these memberships yields
$\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{T}\to\mathrm{C}}
=[m_{ki}^{\mathrm{T}\to\mathrm{C}}]\in[0,1]^{K\times N}$.
Thus, each chunk is assigned membership one in its primary Leiden community.
It can additionally belong to other topic hyperedges when its similarity to
their topic anchors exceeds the threshold $\eta$. The topic-induced hyperedge
anchored by $t_k$ is
\begin{equation}
e_k^{\mathrm{T}\to\mathrm{C}}
=\left\{c_i\in\mathcal{C}\mid
m_{ki}^{\mathrm{T}\to\mathrm{C}}>0\right\}.
\end{equation}

The resulting hyperedge set
$\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{T}\to\mathrm{C}}
=\{e_k^{\mathrm{T}\to\mathrm{C}}\}_{k=1}^{K}$ therefore defines the
topic--chunk hypergraph
$\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{T}\to\mathrm{C}}
=(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{T}\to\mathrm{C}})$.
A chunk may participate in multiple topic hyperedges when it has
sufficiently high similarity to multiple topic anchors.

\section{Layer-wise Asymmetric Index Updates}
\label{app:asymmetric_indexing_details}

This appendix instantiates the asymmetric propagation rule in \Eqref{eq:asymmetric_propagation} for the four document levels. All aggregation weights are inherited from the cross-level membership mappings defined in Section~\ref{sec:hierarchical_hypergraph}. The seed topic-anchor representations used for fuzzy-topic induction are not used during indexing; instead, indexing starts from the initialized topic representations $\mathbf{h}_{t_k}^{(\mathrm{raw})}$.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Topic Level.}
Topics form the root level and therefore receive no parent context. Their raw representations are retained as the final index representations:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_{t_k}^{(\mathrm{final})}
=
\mathbf{h}_{t_k}^{(\mathrm{raw})},
\qquad
k=1,\ldots,K.
\label{eq:topic_final_representation}
\end{equation}

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Chunk Level.}
For each chunk $c_i$, final topic representations provide contextual guidance according to the fuzzy topic--chunk memberships, whereas raw sentence representations provide factual support according to chunk--sentence containment:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{par}}^{(\mathrm{final})}(c_i)
=
\operatorname{norm}\!\left(
\frac{
\sum_{k=1}^{K}
m_{ki}^{\mathrm{T}\to\mathrm{C}}
\mathbf{h}_{t_k}^{(\mathrm{final})}
}{
\sum_{k=1}^{K}m_{ki}^{\mathrm{T}\to\mathrm{C}}
}
\right),
\qquad
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{child}}^{(\mathrm{raw})}(c_i)
=
\operatorname{norm}\!\left(
\frac{
\sum_{j=1}^{J}
m_{ij}^{\mathrm{C}\to\mathrm{S}}
\mathbf{h}_{s_j}^{(\mathrm{raw})}
}{
\sum_{j=1}^{J}m_{ij}^{\mathrm{C}\to\mathrm{S}}
}
\right).
\label{eq:chunk_context_evidence}
\end{equation}
Since $\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{C}\to\mathrm{S}}$ is binary, the second term is the normalized mean of the raw sentence representations contained in $c_i$. The final chunk representation is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_{c_i}^{(\mathrm{final})}
=
\operatorname{Fuse}_{\mathrm{sim}}\!\left(
\mathbf{h}_{c_i}^{(\mathrm{raw})},
\left\{
\mathbf{h}_{c_i}^{(\mathrm{raw})},
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{par}}^{(\mathrm{final})}(c_i),
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{child}}^{(\mathrm{raw})}(c_i)
\right\}
\right).
\label{eq:chunk_final_representation}
\end{equation}

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Sentence Level.}
A sentence may be associated with multiple parent chunks when chunks overlap. Its parent context is therefore aggregated from final chunk representations, while its finer-level evidence is aggregated from raw semantic-unit representations:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{par}}^{(\mathrm{final})}(s_j)
=
\operatorname{norm}\!\left(
\frac{
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
m_{ij}^{\mathrm{C}\to\mathrm{S}}
\mathbf{h}_{c_i}^{(\mathrm{final})}
}{
\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{ij}^{\mathrm{C}\to\mathrm{S}}
}
\right),
\qquad
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{child}}^{(\mathrm{raw})}(s_j)
=
\operatorname{norm}\!\left(
\frac{
\sum_{m=1}^{M}
m_{jm}^{\mathrm{S}\to\mathrm{U}}
\mathbf{h}_{u_m}^{(\mathrm{raw})}
}{
\sum_{m=1}^{M}m_{jm}^{\mathrm{S}\to\mathrm{U}}
}
\right).
\label{eq:sentence_context_evidence}
\end{equation}
Because $m_{jm}^{\mathrm{S}\to\mathrm{U}}$ incorporates the importance score $\rho_m$, more important entities contribute more strongly to the raw factual support of $s_j$. The sentence representation is updated by
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_{s_j}^{(\mathrm{final})}
=
\operatorname{Fuse}_{\mathrm{sim}}\!\left(
\mathbf{h}_{s_j}^{(\mathrm{raw})},
\left\{
\mathbf{h}_{s_j}^{(\mathrm{raw})},
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{par}}^{(\mathrm{final})}(s_j),
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{child}}^{(\mathrm{raw})}(s_j)
\right\}
\right).
\label{eq:sentence_final_representation}
\end{equation}

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Entity Level.}
Entities form the finest document level. Each entity $u_m$ therefore receives only final sentence context and retains its own raw representation:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{par}}^{(\mathrm{final})}(u_m)
=
\operatorname{norm}\!\left(
\frac{
\sum_{j=1}^{J}
m_{jm}^{\mathrm{S}\to\mathrm{U}}
\mathbf{h}_{s_j}^{(\mathrm{final})}
}{
\sum_{j=1}^{J}m_{jm}^{\mathrm{S}\to\mathrm{U}}
}
\right),
\label{eq:entity_parent_context}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_{u_m}^{(\mathrm{final})}
=
\operatorname{Fuse}_{\mathrm{sim}}\!\left(
\mathbf{h}_{u_m}^{(\mathrm{raw})},
\left\{
\mathbf{h}_{u_m}^{(\mathrm{raw})},
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{par}}^{(\mathrm{final})}(u_m)
\right\}
\right).
\label{eq:entity_final_representation}
\end{equation}

The resulting dependency order is
$\mathbf{h}_{t_k}^{(\mathrm{final})}
\rightarrow
\mathbf{h}_{c_i}^{(\mathrm{final})}
\rightarrow
\mathbf{h}_{s_j}^{(\mathrm{final})}
\rightarrow
\mathbf{h}_{u_m}^{(\mathrm{final})}$.
At every update, contextual information is received only from final representations at the coarser level, whereas factual support is supplied only by raw representations at the finer level.

\section{Diagnostic for Same-Entity Different-Sense Disambiguation Across Document Contexts}
\label{app:same_entity_different_sense_disambiguation}

To provide a targeted diagnostic for same-entity different-sense disambiguation across document contexts, we constructed a balanced set of 150 entity pairs from the fixed multi-domain corpus, comprising 75 same-sense pairs and 75 different-sense pairs. Each pair contains two occurrences of the same entity in different document contexts. Each occurrence was used only as a query-time context over the fixed corpus index. We treated different-sense pairs as positive instances and used cosine distance between the two contextualized representations as the disambiguation score. We report AUROC with 95\% confidence intervals obtained from 2,000 stratified bootstrap samples.

We compare a Raw condition, which uses only the static embedding of the target entity, with the Full cascade condition, which contextualizes the target using topic routing and retrieved chunk-, sentence-, and entity-level evidence. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:same_entity_different_sense_disambiguation}, Raw matching cannot distinguish the two classes because identical entities receive identical static embeddings. In contrast, Full cascade achieves an AUROC of 0.774, with a 95\% confidence interval of $[0.691, 0.854]$. Its paired AUROC improvement over Raw is 0.274, with a 95\% bootstrap interval of $[0.191, 0.354]$. These results indicate that query-time cascaded contextualization can distinguish entities used with different meanings in the evaluated corpus.

We independently vary the representations used for the two signals between the raw expanded-query representation $\mathbf{h}_{q}^{(\mathrm{raw})}$ and the corresponding final query representations $\mathbf{h}_{q,\mathrm{C}}^{(\mathrm{final})}$ and

\section{Entity-Mediated Multi-Hop Evidence Expansion}
\label{app:entity_mediated_expansion}

This appendix details the entity-mediated expansion procedure used after
sentence reranking in Section~\ref{sec:method}. The first-stage sentence
evidence is obtained by selecting the highest-ranked sentences according to
\Eqref{eq:sentence_reranking_score}, denoted by
$\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{S}}^{(1)}(q)$.

For each first-stage sentence $s_j\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{S}}^{(1)}(q)$, we
use its extracted entities $\mathcal{U}(s_j)$ to locate unvisited sentences
that mention the same entity. Let $\operatorname{key}(u)$ denote the
normalized textual form of entity $u$. The expansion candidate set is defined
as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{hop}}(q)
=
\left\{
s_{j'}\in\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{S}}^{(1)}(q)
\;\middle|\;
\exists\,s_j\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{S}}^{(1)}(q),\,
u_m\in\mathcal{U}(s_j),\,
u_{m'}\in\mathcal{U}(s_{j'}):
\operatorname{key}(u_m)=\operatorname{key}(u_{m'})
\right\}.
\label{eq:entity_mediated_expansion_candidates}
\end{equation}

Each expanded candidate $s_{j'}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{hop}}(q)$ is scored
using the same chunk-level, sentence-level, and entity-level relevance
signals in Equations~\ref{eq:sentence_relevance_signals} and
\ref{eq:sentence_reranking_score}. To prioritize directly retrieved evidence,
we apply a hop-decay factor $\lambda_{\mathrm{hop}}\in(0,1]$:
\begin{equation}
R_{j'}^{(2)}
=
\lambda_{\mathrm{hop}} R_{j'},
\qquad
s_{j'}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{hop}}(q),
\label{eq:hop_expansion_score}
\end{equation}
where $R_{j'}$ is the reranking score before decay. The highest-ranked
expanded sentences form the second-stage evidence
$\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{S}}^{(2)}(q)$. The final sentence evidence supplied to
generation is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{S}}(q)
=
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{S}}^{(1)}(q)
\cup
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{S}}^{(2)}(q).
\label{eq:multi_hop_sentence_evidence}
\end{equation}

For an $n$-hop expansion, the above procedure is applied iteratively, using the evidence obtained at the previous hop to construct and rerank candidates for the next hop. By linking sentences through shared entities, the expansion can recover evidence located outside the chunks selected during coarse retrieval.

\section{Detailed Experimental Setup}
\label{app:experimental_setup}

\subsection{Datasets}

We use eight document QA datasets: five machine-tool corpora (Mechanical, Hydraulics, Electrical, Maintenance, and System) and three general-domain corpora (Mix, Pathology, and Physics). The machine-tool corpora contain hierarchical technical descriptions and dense domain entities, whereas the three general-domain corpora test transfer under different terminology and document structures. Raw documents are deduplicated and converted into textual contexts. In each evaluation run, questions are generated from newly sampled corpus chunks, and their source contexts are retained as reference evidence.

Mechanical is the controlled benchmark for detailed analysis. In each evaluation run, it uses three 50-question stages: Stage~1 requires direct factual evidence, Stage~2 requires one intermediate evidence connection, and Stage~3 requires two connected evidence links. This progression tests retrieval from local support to compositional multi-hop evidence. Table~\ref{tab:dataset_statistics} reports corpus sizes and question counts per evaluation run.

\subsection{Baselines}

The six criteria are Comprehensiveness, Diversity, Empowerment, Logical, Readability, and Relevance, each scored from 0 to 100. All methods receive the same reference evidence, rubric, and judging prompt (Appendix~\ref{app:llm_judge_scoring_prompt}). We conduct five independent evaluation runs. In each run, we randomly sample different corpus chunks and generate a distinct 50-question evaluation set for each dataset and Mechanical stage. We report the mean $\pm$ standard deviation across the five run-level mean scores. The average score is the mean over the six criteria.
